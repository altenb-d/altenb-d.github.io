Project 02

Fun with Filters and Frequencies

Overview

This project is about what filters do to an image and how that looks in the frequency domain. The first half builds 2D convolution from scratch and uses it with finite difference and Gaussian filters to find edges. The second half uses the same Gaussian as a low-pass filter for three applications: sharpening an image with an unsharp mask, building hybrid images that change when you look at them from further away, and blending two images across a seam with Gaussian and Laplacian stacks.

Part 1.1: Convolutions from scratch

Approach

A convolution flips the kernel horizontally and vertically, slides it over the image and, at every position, multiplies the kernel with the pixels underneath and sums the result. To get an output with the same size as the input ("same" mode), the image is first zero-padded: kh - 1 extra rows and kw - 1 extra columns in total, with kh // 2 rows on top and kw // 2 columns on the left. That split puts the extra pixel of an even-sized kernel such as D_x = [1, -1] on the same side as scipy.signal.convolve2d does, so the results match exactly. 

def zero_padding(image, kernel_height, kernel_width):
    top, left = kernel_height // 2, kernel_width // 2
    padded = np.zeros((image.shape[0] + kernel_height - 1,
                       image.shape[1] + kernel_width - 1))
    padded[top:top + image.shape[0], left:left + image.shape[1]] = image
    return padded

The first version uses four loops: two over the output pixels and two over the kernel entries. 

def convolution_four_loops(image, kernel):
    kernel = np.flip(kernel)
    kernel_height, kernel_width = kernel.shape
    padded = zero_padding(image, kernel_height, kernel_width)
    output = np.zeros(image.shape)
    for i in range(image.shape[0]):
        for j in range(image.shape[1]):
            result = 0
            for k in range(kernel_height):
                for l in range(kernel_width):
                    result += padded[i + k, j + l] * kernel[k, l]
            output[i, j] = result
    return output

The second version replaces the two inner loops with a single numpy operation: it cuts out the window under the kernel and sums its element-wise product with the kernel. 

def convolution_two_loops(image, kernel):
    kernel = np.flip(kernel)
    kernel_height, kernel_width = kernel.shape
    padded = zero_padding(image, kernel_height, kernel_width)
    output = np.zeros(image.shape)
    for i in range(image.shape[0]):
        for j in range(image.shape[1]):
            window = padded[i:i + kernel_height, j:j + kernel_width]
            output[i, j] = np.sum(window * kernel)
    return output

All three give the same result up to floating point rounding. The four-loop version is by far the slowest because every single multiplication is a Python operation. The two-loop version is several times faster because the work inside the loop is one vectorized numpy call instead of 81 Python operations. scipy is faster again by orders of magnitude, since the whole convolution runs in compiled code. This is also why the selfie was resized before timing: at full phone resolution the four-loop version would run for many minutes.

With zero padding, pixels near the border are averaged with zeros that are not part of the image, so the box-filtered result gets a dark frame around the edge. scipy with its default boundary="fill" does exactly the same. Mirroring the image at the border (boundary="symm") avoids this and is used from Part 1.2 on.

Results

The derivative images have negative values, so they are rescaled to [0, 1] for display: gray means no change, bright and dark mean an increase or a decrease in brightness. D_x responds to vertical edges and D_y to horizontal ones.

Part 1.2: Finite difference operator

Convolving the cameraman image with D_x and D_y gives the partial derivatives in x and y. The gradient magnitude combines them as sqrt(dx² + dy²), which is large wherever the brightness changes quickly in any direction. Thresholding the magnitude turns it into a binary edge image.

Choosing the threshold

At 0.1 the grass in the lower half turns into a field of noise and the buildings in the background break up into many small fragments. At 0.3 the grass is clean, but the tripod legs and the outline of the coat start to get gaps. A threshold of 0.2 is the compromise: most of the grass noise is gone while the silhouette, the camera and the tripod stay connected. Some noise in the grass remains, because a two-pixel difference filter reacts to every small change in brightness.

The thin line along the top and right border is not caused by the convolution (the image is mirrored at the border). It is a light strip that is part of the provided cameraman image itself.

Part 1.3: Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) filter

Blur first, then differentiate

The 2D Gaussian is the outer product of a 1D kernel from cv2.getGaussianKernel with itself. With σ = 2 the kernel is 13×13 (±3σ). Blurring the image before taking finite differences removes most of the fine noise, so the same procedure as in 1.2 gives much cleaner edges.

Compared to 1.2, the grass noise is almost completely gone and the edges are smooth, continuous lines instead of jagged pixel steps. The price is that they are thicker and slightly less precisely located, and very fine details disappear together with the noise. Because blurring also lowers the gradient values overall, a much lower threshold (0.05 instead of 0.2) is needed.

One convolution with DoG filters

Since convolution is associative, (I * G) * D_x = I * (G * D_x). Convolving the Gaussian with D_x and D_y first gives two derivative-of-Gaussian filters, and each only has to be applied to the image once.

The largest difference between the gradient magnitudes of both approaches is 1.1e-15, so they give the same result up to floating point rounding, and the two edge images are identical.

Part 2.1: Image sharpening

How the unsharp mask works

A Gaussian filter keeps the low frequencies of an image. Subtracting the blurred image from the original therefore leaves only the high frequencies: edges and fine detail. An image looks sharper when these are stronger, so the unsharp mask adds them back, scaled by α:

f + α(f − f * g) = f * ((1 + α)e − αg)

where e is the unit impulse. The right-hand side shows that the whole operation is a single convolution with one kernel. Its entries sum to 1, so the overall brightness of the image stays the same. All results use σ = 2.

Taj Mahal

Small values of α make the image look crisper without being obviously processed. With larger α the edges get bright and dark halos, and noise and compression artifacts are amplified as much as the real detail. At α = 5 the image looks harsh and artificial.

Own image

The same procedure on a photo of my cat, again with σ = 2.

Evaluation: blur a sharp image and sharpen it again

The sharpened image looks clearly crisper than the blurred one, but it does not get back to the original. Large edges such as the outline of the face and hair regain their contrast, but the finest details like individual hairs and skin texture stay lost. Blurring removed those frequencies almost completely, and the unsharp mask can only amplify frequencies that are still present. Sharpening makes an image look sharper, it cannot recover information.

Part 2.2: Hybrid images

Approach

A hybrid image is the low frequencies of one image plus the high frequencies of another. Up close, the high frequencies dominate perception. From further away the eye can no longer resolve them, and only the low-frequency image remains. The low-pass is a Gaussian blur with σ₁. The high-pass is the image minus its Gaussian blur with σ₂. Before filtering, the two images are aligned with the provided starter code by clicking two corresponding points in each (the eyes). This matters because the two interpretations only fuse if their shapes overlap.

The cutoff of each filter can be expressed as the frequency where the Gaussian's response drops to one half, f = √(ln 2 / 2) / (πσ) ≈ 0.19 / σ cycles per pixel. A larger σ₁ keeps fewer frequencies of the low-pass image, a smaller σ₂ keeps only the finest details of the high-pass image.

Derek and Nutmeg: full process

Aligning on the eyes rotates, scales and shifts the images so that the two faces overlap. The black areas are filled in by the alignment code.

The log magnitude of the Fourier transform shows the same process in the frequency domain. The center of each plot is the lowest frequency and the edges are the highest. The low-passed image only keeps a bright spot in the center, the high-passed image has its center removed, and the hybrid combines the center of one with the outer region of the other.

σ₁ = 7 (cutoff ≈ 0.027 cycles/pixel) blurs Derek enough that his face is not distracting up close, while it is still clearly recognizable from a distance. σ₂ = 4 (cutoff ≈ 0.047) keeps Nutmeg's fur and whiskers, which are what makes the cat recognizable up close.

Me and a koala

The first attempt used the images the other way around, with my face as the high-pass image. That did not work: my face has strong edges that stayed visible at every distance, and the almost white koala as the low-pass image made the whole result look washed out. Swapping them works much better, because a face is recognizable even when blurred, while the koala's fur is full of fine texture. With σ₂ = 4 the koala's large dark nose was still visible from far away. At σ₂ = 2 (cutoff ≈ 0.094) only the fur remains, and from a distance my face takes over.

Me and a pixel-art character

This pair needed very different values. Only the character's head is used: the alignment scales the image up so that its eyes land on mine. The pixel-art face consists of hard, high-contrast block edges, which carry a lot of energy in both the middle and high frequencies. With σ₂ between 3 and 5 it dominated the hybrid at every distance. Only after lowering σ₂ step by step to 0.8 (cutoff ≈ 0.23) did the blocks shrink to a thin grid of edges that disappears from far away. A smaller σ₁ = 3 keeps more of my eyes and mouth, so my face can hold its own against those edges from a distance.

Part 2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

A stack works like a pyramid without the downsampling: every level has the full image size. Level k of the Gaussian stack is the original blurred with σ = 2 · 2^(k−1), so the blur doubles from level to level, like a pyramid whose resolution halves each time. Each level of the Laplacian stack is the difference between two neighbouring Gaussian levels, a band-pass image that contains only the frequencies between their cutoffs. The last level is the most blurred Gaussian level, so summing the whole Laplacian stack gives back the original image exactly. Six levels are used throughout.

Part 2.4: Multiresolution blending

Approach

Cutting two images along a hard seam leaves a visible edge. Blurring the seam by one fixed amount does not work either: a wide transition makes fine detail look ghosted, a narrow one leaves a visible step in large, smooth areas. Multiresolution blending (Burt and Adelson, 1983) blends every frequency band separately. For each level k the two Laplacian levels are combined with the matching level of the mask's Gaussian stack:

blended_k = LA_k · (1 − G_k) + LB_k · G_k

Fine details are joined along an almost sharp seam, while low frequencies such as overall color and brightness are mixed over a wide region. Summing all blended levels gives the result. Color images are blended per channel with the same mask.

Oraple

Apple and tomato

The two fruits had very different shapes: the apple is wide and flat, the tomato tall and egg-shaped. Before blending, the apple was scaled to the tomato's width and height and both were shifted so their centers line up. The seam sits slightly left of the center, where the outlines of both fruits match best. Without these steps the result had a clear step at the top and bottom of the seam, which no amount of blending can hide, since blending only smooths colors and textures, not mismatched shapes.

Eye in a hand (irregular mask)

The eye photo was scaled, rotated and moved so that the eye sits in the middle of the palm, and the elliptical mask around it was moved the same way. Because the low frequencies are mixed over a wide area, the reddish skin around the eye fades into the lighter skin of the palm instead of ending at the edge of the ellipse, while the eyelashes stay sharp.

Laplacian stack of the blend

At level 0 the mask is almost sharp, so the fine texture of the eyelashes and the lines of the palm are cut cleanly along the ellipse. At level 4 the mask is very wide, and the low-frequency colors of both images overlap over a large region. This is where the difference in skin tone is evened out.

What I learned

The most important thing I learned is how much of an image's appearance depends on which frequencies you look at. Edges, sharpness, hybrid images and seamless blending are all the same idea underneath: separate the image into frequency bands with a Gaussian and treat the bands differently. The hybrid images made this very concrete: the same picture shows two different things depending only on how far away you stand.
