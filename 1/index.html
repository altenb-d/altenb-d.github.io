Project 01

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Overview

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in three exposures per scene, one through a blue filter, one through green and one through red, all recorded onto a single glass plate. The digitized plates are stacked vertically in that order. To recover a color photograph the plate has to be cut into three channels and the channels have to be shifted back on top of each other, since the three exposures were never perfectly registered.

This page walks through a single-scale search first, then the pyramid that makes the same idea work on the full-resolution scans, and finally an extension that aligns on image gradients instead of raw pixel values.

Part 1: Single-scale alignment

Approach

The plate is divided into three equal strips by integer division of its height. Any leftover rows at the bottom are discarded so that all three channels have exactly the same shape. Green and red are then each aligned against blue.

The search itself is exhaustive. For every displacement in a window of [-15, 15] pixels in both directions the moving channel is shifted with np.roll, scored against the reference, and the best-scoring displacement is kept. Because np.roll wraps around, the strip that re-enters on the opposite side is meaningless; both images are therefore cropped by 10% per side before scoring, which also removes the bright plate borders. Those borders sit in the same place in all three channels, so leaving them in would let the search optimize for aligning the borders rather than the photograph. Ten percent per side is enough to clear the borders on every plate in the set. A larger crop of 20% was tried and did not improve any of the results.

Metrics

L2 (Euclidean distance) treats each image as a long vector and measures the straight-line distance between them: the difference is squared, summed over all pixels, and square-rooted. It is cheap, but it assumes the two channels have comparable brightness, because a constant offset between them adds to the distance at every single pixel.

NCC (normalized cross-correlation) removes that assumption. Both images are mean-subtracted and divided by their norm, and the dot product of the two resulting unit vectors is taken. Subtracting the mean makes the score indifferent to a brightness offset and dividing by the norm makes it indifferent to a contrast factor, so NCC responds to the pattern in the image rather than to absolute intensities. A perfect match scores 1.

Both are implemented so that a higher value means a better match. The L2 result is negated, which lets the search routine simply take the maximum regardless of which metric it was handed.

Results

All three low-resolution plates align cleanly, and L2 and NCC agree on the displacement for each of them. Each takes well under a second.

Part 2: Multi-scale pyramid alignment

Why the single-scale search is not enough

The full-size .tif plates are roughly ten times larger in each dimension, so the displacements are roughly ten times larger too, up to about 180 pixels in the results below. Widening the search window to cover that range is quadratic: a radius of 180 means over 130,000 candidate positions, each scored on a full-resolution image. That is far too slow.

Approach

Instead the image is repeatedly halved and the search is done on the smallest version, where a displacement of 180 pixels has shrunk to around 11 and the [-15, 15] window covers it comfortably. The estimate found there is then carried back up.

Concretely, align_pyramid recurses: it downsamples both channels and calls itself with one level fewer, stopping either when the level budget runs out or when the channel is under 400 pixels tall. On these plates the size threshold is always the binding one: a channel of 3250 rows is halved four times, to 1625, 812, 406 and finally 203, so the budget of five is never exhausted.

On the way back up, each returned estimate is doubled to account for the change in scale, the moving channel is shifted by that amount, and only a small correction of ±3 pixels is searched for at the current resolution. The total displacement is the doubled estimate plus that correction. The expensive full-radius search therefore happens exactly once, on the smallest image; every level above it costs 49 comparisons.

Downsampling applies a Gaussian blur before discarding every second row and column. Without that low-pass step, high frequencies in the original alias into the smaller image, and the coarse levels then report displacements that do not correspond to the real shift, an error which is subsequently doubled at every level on the way back up.

Failure case: church

One plate fails with L2 on raw pixels. The red channel of church.tif comes back at a horizontal displacement of 267 pixels, an order of magnitude larger than anything else in the set, and the result shows heavy red ghosting along the church walls and roofs.

The plate is dominated by a large expanse of water and sky with very little structure. Over most of the image there is simply nothing for a pixelwise distance to lock onto, so a spurious minimum on one of the coarse pyramid levels scores better than the correct one. Because every estimate is doubled on the way up, an error of a handful of pixels at the bottom of the pyramid becomes a few hundred at the top, and a correction window of ±3 cannot recover from it.

Swapping the metric

Running the same pyramid with NCC instead of L2 fixes church, whose red comes back at (58, -4), but breaks emir.tif, which lands at (141, -205). The two metrics fail on different plates, so each configuration has exactly one defective image out of fourteen. NCC is also roughly three times slower, at 20–30 seconds per plate against 8–12 for L2.

Part 3: Images from the collection

Three further plates were taken from the Library of Congress collection, in each case the full-resolution .tif scan rather than the restored color version, and run through the same pipeline with the same parameters as everything above.

Metric: L2 on gradient magnitudes, 5 pyramid levels, 10% crop.

The bust is the plate with the least color information of the three: white plaster against a dark niche against a light wall, so all the structure is in brightness rather than in hue. It aligns without trouble, which is not surprising. The silhouette of the bust against the niche is a strong edge that shows up in all three channels regardless of how much light each filter let through, and the carved curls of the hair give the search plenty of fine detail to settle on.

The camel plate shows the limit of the translation model rather than a failure of the search. The man, the sacks and the tethering ropes are sharp, but there is red and green fringing around the camel's muzzle and ears. The animal moved between the three exposures, and no single shift of the whole channel can undo a subject that was in a different place each time the shutter opened. Correcting it would require a model that allows different parts of the image to move independently.

Bells & Whistles: Aligning on gradients

Motivation

Neither metric handles the whole set on its own: L2 loses church, NCC loses emir. Both failures have the same root. The three channels are exposures through different filters, so a given surface does not have the same brightness in all three, and a metric comparing raw intensities is comparing two pictures that genuinely do not look alike. emir.tif is the textbook case: the robe is bright in one channel and dark in another.

Edges do not have that problem. Wherever the robe meets the wall there is a discontinuity in all three channels, whatever the absolute levels on either side happen to be. So instead of comparing intensities, the alignment compares gradient magnitudes: a Sobel derivative in x and one in y, combined as the length of the gradient vector. The search itself is unchanged, and the displacement it finds is applied to the original channels. Only what gets scored is different.

Aligning on gradients fixes both problem plates at once, and no image in the set is left with a visible defect. The more interesting result is that L2 and NCC now return identical displacements on all fourteen provided plates. Once the comparison is made on edges rather than intensities, the property that distinguishes NCC, its indifference to brightness and contrast differences between the channels, no longer buys anything, because the gradient magnitude has already discarded the absolute levels. The choice of metric stops mattering, so L2 is the better pick: same answer, about a third of the running time.

Implementation notes

All images are converted to float in [0, 1] on load, with the scale factor taken from the file's dtype rather than from its contents: the JPEGs are 8-bit and the TIFFs 16-bit, and normalizing by the maximum present in each image would put them on different scales. One fixed parameter set is used for every plate: a search radius of 15, five pyramid levels, a correction radius of 3 and a 10% crop. The five-level budget is therefore an upper bound rather than the number of halvings that actually happen. Four is what the size threshold allows on the full-resolution scans.
